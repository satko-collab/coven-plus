# COVEN+

*Coordinated & Orchestrated Voices for Expert Navigation + Meta-Interpretive Reasoning Layer*

## 1. Task contract

TRANSFORM my request into a structured brief, then execute it.

Use the language of my task. Help me think rather than replace my judgement. Do not invent citations. Distinguish facts, assumptions, values, preferences, and unknowns.

- TASK: [insert the request]
- Purpose / decision: [what this will be used for]
- Audience: [who needs the result]
- Constraints: [time, policy, resources, country, model]
- Useful output format: [what can be acted on]

## 2. Meta-Reading

Before answering, show a short section called **Meta-Reading** in 3–5 lines. State:

1. what output would actually help;
2. what expertise is most relevant;
3. what format is most useful;
4. what level of detail is enough.

Show only a concise task interpretation — not private chain-of-thought. Do not repeat this section later.

## 3. Panel design

Create four distinct voices. For each voice specify:

- its role or memorable name;
- what it protects;
- what evidence it trusts;
- its likely blind spot;
- one question or falsifier that could change its mind.

Use the roles I provide. If I provide none, use: domain generalist, methodologist, risk analyst, and exactly one devil’s advocate. The devil’s advocate challenges claims, not people, and does not manufacture opposition.

## 4. Independent first pass

Before seeing the other voices’ answers, each voice gives an independent first pass containing:

- its recommendation;
- assumptions;
- reasons and available evidence;
- the main uncertainty;
- provisional **p** and **a** marks, each with a one-line reason.

Treat this independence as a useful simulation, not as independent evidence.

## 5. Cross-examination and revision

Cross-examination: each voice must:

- name the strongest claim made by another voice;
- challenge one material claim with a counterexample, missing stakeholder, or verification request;
- state what evidence would change its mind.

Revision: each voice says what it kept, changed, or rejected after hearing the others. Do not force consensus. Preserve material disagreement and minority insights.

## 6. Synthesis and benchmark

A Narrator synthesizes the panel into:

1. agreements;
2. material conflicts;
3. resolutions or unresolved choices;
4. unique and minority points;
5. a verification checklist;
6. a recommendation with alternatives.

Separate facts, assumptions, values, preferences, and unknowns. Compare the panel’s result with a simple direct-answer baseline and with my stated criteria. Spend more attention on high-stakes uncertainty; do not claim undefined weights or invisible budgeting calculations.

## 7. p/a quality audit

Finish with two public working marks in **[0,1]**:

- **p = epistemic confidence:** support from evidence, sources, logic, and current knowledge;
- **a = adequacy:** fit to the aim, audience, format, language, constraints, and required depth.

For each mark give one reason, the largest uncertainty or mismatch, and the next check or correction.

- If **p < 0.70**, name the uncertainty and verification route.
- If **a < 0.70**, name the mismatch and correction.
- If **p** and **a** diverge, explain why.

These are working judgements, not calibrated probabilities or measurements.

## Default output order

Meta-Reading → independent voices → cross-examination → revisions → Narrator synthesis → recommendation and alternatives → p/a audit.

## Effort switch

Use **FULL mode** when the decision is consequential, contested, expensive, or hard to reverse.

Use **LIGHT mode**—or a direct prompt—when the task is routine, reversible, and easily checked. In LIGHT mode, keep three voices, one challenge round, a short synthesis, and the p/a audit.

Always ask whether the added complexity changed the answer, made claims more checkable, or merely produced more text.

## Methodological caution

Several voices generated by one model are not independent evidence. A devil’s advocate should test claims rather than manufacture opposition. Agreement can reflect shared blind spots. Multi-agent debate has not reliably beaten simpler prompting strategies across evaluations without careful tuning, so compare the protocol with a direct-answer baseline and use the added complexity only when it improves a human-defined criterion.

Further reading: Smit, A. P., Grinsztajn, N., Duckworth, P., Barrett, T. D., & Pretorius, A. (2024). *Should we be going MAD? A Look at Multi-Agent Debate Strategies for LLMs.* Proceedings of ICML, PMLR 235, 45883–45905. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/smit24a.html
